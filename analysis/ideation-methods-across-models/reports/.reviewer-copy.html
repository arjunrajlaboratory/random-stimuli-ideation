Pushing a language model past its obvious ideas gave the research ideas a model judge rated best

      Abstract

      Language models brainstorm in a rut: asked for research ideas, they tend to return the same standard experiments. We tested nine ways of prompting for ideas on two cell-biology research questions, using three models of different size (Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5). Seven prompts were new. Each prompt and model produced eighty ideas, 2160 in all. A judge model (Opus 5.5) scored every idea for novelty and usefulness without knowing where it came from, after a rewrite that stripped analogies and other framing. Our main measure counts distinct useful-novel ideas: ideas rated at least four out of five on both scales, with near-duplicates merged.

      The plain prompt gave 0 distinct useful-novel ideas for every model. The best prompt, critique and revise, showed the model its own plain-prompt ideas, said an expert found them obvious, and asked for replacements. It gave 34 of 240, and a related prompt that has the model list the obvious ideas first and then avoid them gave 25. Of the seven new prompts, 5 beat the plain prompt after correction for multiple comparisons, but only critique and revise beat the stronger baseline, the best prompt from an earlier study (an instruction to borrow from an unrelated field), by 18 ideas. The gains were largest for Opus 5.5: critique and revise added 19 distinct useful-novel ideas for it, against 10 for Sonnet 5.5 and 5 for Haiku 5.5. A second judge (Sonnet 5.5) agreed on the best and worst prompts. In an exploratory follow-up, an unrelated poem or a list of random words that every idea had to use beat the plain prompt but not the earlier unrelated-field instruction. The main caveat is that every score comes from a language-model judge; no scientist has rated these ideas yet.

      Problem statement

      Ask a language model for ten research ideas and you get a sensible list. Ask again and you get a largely overlapping list. This repetition, often called mode collapse, means a scientist using a model to widen their thinking mostly hears the textbook answers back. An earlier study in this repository asked whether unrelated material (poems, random words, essays) placed before the prompt fixes this. It did not. The one prompt that helped was a single sentence telling the model that each idea must borrow from a field unrelated to the question (we call this the unrelated-domain instruction). That study had three limits: one model, one research question, and a winning prompt designed in the fourth of five rounds of trying things, which makes it an exploratory result.

      This report asks a broader question. Which ways of asking for ideas make a model produce more ideas that are both new and worth doing, and does the answer depend on the model? We designed seven new prompts, each aimed at a specific reason a model might fall back on the obvious, and compared them with the plain prompt and with the unrelated-domain instruction. We tested every prompt on three models of different size and on two research questions.

      The measure that matters is whether a prompt yields ideas a scientist would find both surprising and feasible. We approximate this with a judge model that scores each idea for novelty (would an expert have thought of it?) and usefulness (could a typical lab do it within a year or two, and would the result be informative?). A useful-novel idea scores at least four out of five on both. We ask each model for ideas many times, each time in a fresh, separate request with no memory of the others (a call, which returns five ideas). Because a model can repeat one good idea in call after call, we count distinct useful-novel ideas: useful-novel ideas after merging near-duplicates. A good answer is a prompt that raises this count well above the plain prompt, ideally for every model.

      Methods

      In outline: each model answered each prompt in many separate, fresh calls, a rewrite step removed decorative language, a judge model scored every idea blind, and statistical tests compared prompts by shuffling whole calls between them. The supplement gives the full wording of every prompt and all parameters. The design, prompts, outcome and tests were written down before any idea was generated (the pre-registration is in the analysis folder); the analyses labeled exploratory below were added after seeing the results.

      Nine prompts

      Every prompt asks for five concrete ideas (analyses, measurements or experiments), each with a short title and one or two sentences of description. They differ in what comes before or around that request.

      Plain prompt
      The request alone. This is the baseline.
      Unrelated-domain instruction
      Each idea must borrow an analogy, structure, principle or pattern from a domain unrelated to the question. This was the best prompt in the earlier study; here it is a pre-specified replication.
      Assigned parallel problem
      The model first lists eight problems from eight different fields that share the question's structure. Each later call receives one of them and must transfer a method from it. This keeps the earlier study's most novel approach but forces variety, because in that study the model picked the same parallel (magnetic hysteresis) nearly every time.
      List the obvious, then avoid it
      The model first lists the ten ideas most experts would propose, then gives five ideas that are none of them.
      Overgenerate, then select
      The model brainstorms twenty one-line candidates, then picks and develops the five that best combine being non-obvious with being feasible.
      Invert an assumption
      The model lists the hidden assumptions in the question and in how the field usually tests it; each idea must test or exploit the failure of a different one.
      Assigned constraint
      Each call gets one constraint from a fixed list of eight, such as using only data that are already public, or using no genetic perturbation, and every idea must respect it.
      Critique and revise
      A second, fresh call shows the model the five ideas it gave to the plain prompt in the matching call, says a senior expert finds them the ideas everyone would propose first, and asks for five less obvious but feasible replacements.
      Future retrospective
      The model imagines it is 2036 and that five landmark papers each settled the question in a way that surprised the field, and describes what each one measured or did.

      Models, questions and requests

      The three generators were Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5, the small, medium and large models of the same family. The two research questions were the earlier study's question, "What analyses or measurements would best test whether AP-1 acts as a cellular memory mechanism?" (the cellular-memory question; AP-1 is a family of gene-regulating proteins), and a new one from a different part of cell biology, "What measurements or analyses would best reveal why a small fraction of genetically identical cancer cells survive targeted drug treatment?" (the drug-tolerance or persister question). Each prompt was sent to each model eight times per question, every time as a fresh call with no memory of the others, for 432 calls and 2160 ideas. A condition of the design (one question, model and prompt) therefore holds forty ideas, and each model and prompt holds eighty across the two questions. Two-step prompts used the same model for both steps.

      Removing decoration, then judging blind

      Prompts that ask for analogies produce ideas dressed in borrowed vocabulary ("Weibull reliability analysis: treating cell death as material fracture"). A judge, or an embedding model, can mistake that vocabulary for new science. So before judging, Sonnet 5.5 rewrote every idea in plain scientific language, keeping the experiment and removing analogy, metaphor and numbered framing labels such as "Paper … (hypothetical)". The rewriter saw ideas in shuffled batches and was never told which prompt or model wrote them. We call the result the stripped text; all primary results use it.

      Opus 5.5 then scored each stripped idea for novelty and usefulness on one-to-five scales, in shuffled batches of twenty that mixed prompts and models, with opaque labels. It read every idea twice in different batches; the two readings agreed with rank correlations of 0.83 for novelty and 0.73 for usefulness. We average the two readings. Because Opus 5.5 is both a generator and the judge, we also had Sonnet 5.5 judge every stripped idea once, and had Opus 5.5 judge the original, unstripped wording once.

      Counting distinct useful-novel ideas

      Within each condition, useful-novel ideas are grouped by meaning: we embed each stripped idea as a vector (a sentence embedding model) and merge ideas whose vectors are very similar (cosine similarity of at least 0.75, average-linkage clustering). Each group counts once, and the counts are summed over questions. The worked example below follows one idea through these steps. In its condition, 14 ideas were useful-novel and they formed 10 groups; the largest group shows three phrasings of the same experiment (single-cell apoptotic priming) that count as one.

      Follow-up: unrelated material

      After the main results were in, we added two prompts from the earlier study as an exploratory follow-up. The model received an unrelated poem (eight public-domain poems, one per call) or a list of sixty random dictionary words, with the instruction that each idea must draw on this material, for example through an analogy, structure, principle or pattern. This required wording is the only form in which the earlier study's model used such material at all. Both prompts ran on all three models and both questions (96 calls, 480 ideas) and went through the same stripping and primary judge. To leave the main study's numbers untouched, the new ideas were judged in their own batches, each mixing ten new ideas with ten already-judged ideas from the main study. Those anchor ideas came back with scores close to their original ones (rank correlations of 0.88 for novelty and 0.8 for usefulness; average shifts of 0.18 and -0.07 points), so the judge's scale held, though the small rise in novelty may make the follow-up counts slightly generous. The second judge model and the original-wording run do not cover these two prompts.

      Statistical tests

      Ideas from the same call are not independent, so the call is the unit of every test. To compare two prompts we pool their calls within each question and model, shuffle which calls belong to which prompt 5000 times, and ask how often a shuffled difference is as large as the real one (a two-sided permutation test). Adjusted p-values use the Holm correction: over the three models for the replication test, and over the seven new prompts for each comparison of new prompts with a baseline.

      Results

      In the main analysis the plain prompt produced no distinct useful-novel idea; five new prompts did significantly better

      If prompting does not matter, every prompt should yield about the same number of distinct useful-novel ideas. It does matter. The plain prompt yielded 0 distinct useful-novel ideas out of 240, across all three models and both questions Figure. Its ideas were judged useful (mean 4.08 out of five) but not novel (mean 1.72). Pooled over models, 5 of the seven new prompts beat it (p-values adjusted for the seven comparisons with the plain prompt):

      • critique and revise, +34 (p = 0.0014);
      • list the obvious, then avoid it, +25 (p = 0.0014);
      • overgenerate, then select, +17 (p = 0.0014);
      • invert an assumption, +13 (p = 0.0016);
      • future retrospective, +13 (p = 0.0018).

      The assigned parallel problem (+7, p = 0.122) and the assigned constraint (+1, p = 1.000) did not. Every new prompt raised judged novelty over the plain prompt (largest adjusted p = 0.0014), but shifts in the mean scores do not by themselves explain which prompts cleared both bars: the assigned parallel problem raised novelty by +0.96 points and failed, while overgenerate, then select raised it by only +0.8 and succeeded. The pattern held on both questions: critique and revise gave the most distinct useful-novel ideas on each, and the plain prompt gave none on either (Table).

      The two prompts that show the model its default ideas gave the most distinct useful-novel ideas

      The two strongest prompts share one feature: they put the obvious ideas in front of the model and ask it to go elsewhere. Critique and revise does this with the model's own earlier answers; list the obvious, then avoid it has the model write the obvious list itself. Critique and revise was the only new prompt that beat the unrelated-domain instruction from the earlier study, by 18 distinct useful-novel ideas (p = 0.042, adjusted for the seven comparisons with the instruction). List the obvious, then avoid it was ahead by 9 but not significantly (p = 0.531). The assigned constraint fell significantly behind the instruction (-15, p = 0.0014).

      The examples above show what this looks like. Critique and revise moved Opus 5.5 off lineage barcoding and pooled screens toward ideas such as allele-resolved readout of the rare high-expressing state, sorting cells and fitting how fast they relax back, and changing the rate at which cells enter the tolerant state without touching resistance genes. Critique and revise costs a second call, since it needs the plain answer first; list the obvious, then avoid it gets a similar effect in one call for Opus 5.5 but not for Sonnet 5.5.

      The earlier study's instruction replicated overall and was significant on its own only for the largest model

      The unrelated-domain instruction was the earlier study's best prompt, found after several rounds; here we specified in advance that it should beat the plain prompt. Pooled over models it did, by 16 distinct useful-novel ideas (p = 0.0002), raising novelty by 1.47 points and changing usefulness by -0.56. Tested model by model, with p-values adjusted for the three models, the gain was significant for Opus 5.5 (+9, p = 0.012) but not for Sonnet 5.5 (+4, p = 0.090) or Haiku 5.5 (+3, p = 0.231). The earlier study used Sonnet 5.5, where the effect here is smaller and not significant on its own; with only eighty ideas per model and prompt, small effects are hard to detect.

      The largest model gained the most from the new prompts

      If prompt design works the same way regardless of model, each prompt should help the three models by similar amounts. The gains were in fact much larger for Opus 5.5, although we did not test the difference between models formally and the smaller models' tests have little power. With Opus 5.5, 5 of the seven new prompts beat the plain prompt on their own (p-values adjusted for the seven prompts within that model): critique and revise and list the obvious, then avoid it (+19 and +19), overgenerate, then select (+14), invert an assumption, and future retrospective. With Sonnet 5.5, 1 did: critique and revise (+10, p = 0.0098). With Haiku 5.5, 0 did, although critique and revise and list the obvious, then avoid it each produced 5 distinct useful-novel ideas of 80, against 0 for the plain prompt.

      The models differ in how their new ideas fall short. Under critique and revise, Haiku 5.5's ideas were judged nearly as novel as Opus 5.5's (mean novelty 4.06 against 4.31) but less useful (3.05 against 3.61), so fewer cleared both bars, as in the examples above. Sonnet 5.5's ideas were judged the least novel of the three models for 7 of the nine prompts. The order of prompts was similar for Haiku 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 and less so between Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5: rank correlations of the gains over the plain prompt were 0.84 between Haiku 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5, 0.7 between Haiku 5.5 and Opus 5.5, and 0.4 between Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5. Critique and revise ranked first or tied for first for all three.

      Most prompts traded usefulness for novelty; overgenerate, then select did not

      A prompt that pushes a model away from standard experiments could simply push it toward impractical ones. For most prompts the judge saw exactly that trade Figure. Pooled over models, critique and revise changed novelty by +2.25 points and usefulness by -0.64; list the obvious, then avoid it changed novelty by +1.96 and usefulness by -0.56. Overgenerate, then select was the exception: it raised novelty by 0.8 with no detectable loss of usefulness (-0.03, p = 0.421). For Opus 5.5, its ideas were as useful as the plain prompt's (means 4.22 and 4.29). Selecting from a longer list against an explicit feasibility criterion may keep feasibility in view; the design cannot separate the two. The assigned constraint lost almost as much usefulness (-0.58) as critique and revise while gaining far less novelty, plausibly because constraints such as a single time point per sample limit what an idea can test; we did not analyze the constraints one by one.

      A second judge, the original wording, and a lower cutoff agreed on the best and worst prompts

      Three checks address the obvious worries about a model judge. First, Opus 5.5 judging Opus 5.5's ideas might favor its own style. Sonnet 5.5 judging the same stripped ideas agreed with the primary judge idea by idea (rank correlation 0.82 for novelty and 0.75 for usefulness), ranked critique and revise first and the plain prompt last (27 and 0 distinct useful-novel ideas of 240), and found 7 of the seven new prompts significantly better than the plain prompt, against five for the primary judge Figure. It also rated Opus 5.5's ideas the most novel of the three models (mean 3.31, against 2.68 for Haiku 5.5 and 2.48 for its own model), so the advantage of Opus 5.5 is not only self-preference by the primary judge.

      Second, stripping could have hidden or created differences. Opus 5.5 judging the original wording gave higher counts for every prompt, including a few for the plain prompt, but the same best and worst prompts (critique and revise 52, plain prompt 5, of 240 each). The plain prompt's zero in the main analysis therefore depends partly on judging the stripped text. Third, the cutoff of four is arbitrary. With a cutoff of 3.5 the same two prompts lead and the same two trail; at 4.5 almost no idea from any prompt qualifies, too few to rank prompts (supplement). Counts differ in level between the three runs (a single reading is noisier than the average of two, which pulls extreme scores toward the middle), so only the order of prompts should be compared across runs.

      Exploratory: the two best prompts found mostly different ideas

      These two analyses were added after seeing the results. First, if critique and revise and list the obvious, then avoid it merely reached the same "second tier" of ideas, running both would add little. For Opus 5.5, each gave 19 distinct useful-novel ideas (of 80) and together they gave 34 (of 160), so most were different. Across all eight non-plain prompts (640 ideas per model), Opus 5.5 produced 69 distinct useful-novel ideas, against 16 for Haiku 5.5 and 16 for Sonnet 5.5. Running several prompts may therefore add more breadth than running one prompt more often, though we did not compare the two at equal numbers of calls.

      Second, cost. Using the per-call costs reported by the command-line tool (equivalent to pay-per-use pricing), a plain call cost about $0.0008 for Haiku 5.5 and $0.0623 for Opus 5.5. Per distinct useful-novel idea, critique and revise cost $0.008 with Haiku 5.5 and $0.164 with Opus 5.5, counting the plain call it depends on. Haiku 5.5 gives far fewer good ideas, and a person still has to read past its weaker ones, which this figure does not count.

      Follow-up: requiring the model to use a poem or random words beat the plain prompt but not the earlier instruction

      The earlier study found that unrelated material changed a model's ideas only when every idea had to use it. Here, in an exploratory follow-up, a required poem gave 12 distinct useful-novel ideas of 240 and required random words gave 9. Both beat the plain prompt (+12, p = 0.0024; +9, p = 0.0086; adjusted over the two follow-up prompts), and neither differed significantly from the unrelated-domain instruction (-4, p = 0.506; -7, p = 0.328). Compared with that instruction, their ideas were less novel (by -0.81 and -0.76 points) and more useful (by +0.4 and +0.39): the material pulled ideas a little away from the standard ones and cost little usefulness, but less than letting the model pick its own unrelated field. Nearly all of the gain came from Opus 5.5 (11 and 9 of 80); Sonnet 5.5 gave 0 and 0. Their totals fall in the middle of the main study's range Figure, below the prompts that show the model its own default ideas.

      Conclusions

      The question was which ways of asking a model for research ideas yield more ideas that are both new and worth doing. On two cell-biology questions and three models, the answer was clear in direction. The plain prompt yielded no distinct useful-novel idea in the main analysis. Prompts that confront the model with its own default ideas (critique and revise, and list the obvious, then avoid it) yielded the most. Critique and revise was the only prompt to beat the earlier study's unrelated-domain instruction, and that instruction itself replicated when pooled over models. Overgenerate, then select was the one prompt that raised novelty without lowering usefulness. In the follow-up, unrelated material that each idea had to use landed in the middle of the field, no better than simply asking for an unrelated field.

      The size of the gain differed by model. Most of it came from Opus 5.5. Among the smaller models, only critique and revise with Sonnet 5.5 reached significance on its own; we did not formally test whether the models differ. A plausible reading, which this study does not test directly, is that a larger model knows more of the less-traveled experiments and can reach them once told the obvious ones are off the table, while a smaller model asked for less obvious ideas tends to produce less feasible ones.

      Main caveat

      Every quality score in this report comes from language-model judges. The judges agree with each other and with themselves, and a second judge model picks the same best and worst prompts, but no scientist has rated these ideas. A model judge may share the generators' blind spots, may reward ideas that sound unusual more than ideas a lab would actually run, and is one of the three generators. The usefulness penalties in particular need checking by people who run these experiments.

      Further limits: both questions come from cell biology and from the same research area; each model and prompt produced eighty ideas from one run, so modest effects for Haiku 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 could be missed; critique and revise uses two calls and so more compute than the single-call prompts; and the exploratory analyses (complementarity and cost) were not planned in advance.

      Next steps

      • Blind ratings by scientists. Build a rating sheet from the plain prompt, critique and revise, list the obvious, then avoid it, and overgenerate, then select, for the largest and smallest models, and have lab members rate novelty and usefulness without knowing the source.
      • Combine the strongest prompts. Test a pipeline that runs overgenerate, then select, followed by a critique-and-revise round, and check whether it keeps usefulness while raising novelty.
      • Use it in practice. Add a critique-and-revise step to the repository's persona-based brainstorming skill (a tool that has the model brainstorm in the voice of several expert personas) and compare its output with the current version on a live project question.
      • Test outside cell biology. Repeat the design on questions from a field where the judge models know less, to see whether the ranking holds.

      Supplement

      Prompt wording

      Every prompt except the future retrospective and critique and revise is built around the plain request: "Research question: [question]. Propose five distinct, concrete ideas (analyses, measurements, or experiments) that a research lab could pursue to address this question. For each idea give a short title and a description of one or two sentences." (The number is a word only in this report; the prompts use the numeral.) The additions are:

      Unrelated-domain instruction (before)
      "Each of your ideas must draw on a domain unrelated to the research question below, for example through an analogy, a structure, a principle, or a pattern borrowed from that domain, while still being a concrete, scientifically sound way to address the question."
      Assigned parallel problem (before)
      The field, the parallel problem and how that field studies it, then: "Each of your ideas must transfer a specific method, measurement logic, or principle from how that field studies its problem, while still being a concrete, scientifically sound way to address the research question." The parallels came from one earlier call per model and question that was asked for eight parallel problems from eight different fields outside biology and medicine; a few of the fields chosen (ecology, entomology) are arguably biological.
      List the obvious, then avoid it (after)
      "Before proposing your ideas, list in the obvious field the ten ideas that most experts would propose first for this question (one line each). Then propose your five ideas: none of them may be one of the obvious ideas or a minor variant of one. They must still be concrete and scientifically sound."
      Overgenerate, then select (after)
      "Work in two steps. First, in the candidates field, brainstorm twenty candidate ideas, one line each, covering as wide a range as you can. Then select the five candidates that best combine being non-obvious with being feasible and informative, and develop those as your ideas."
      Invert an assumption (after)
      "Before proposing your ideas, list in the assumptions field the hidden assumptions built into this question and into the way the field usually tests it. Then propose your five ideas: each must test, exploit, or work around the failure of a different one of those assumptions, while still being a concrete, scientifically sound way to address the question."
      Assigned constraint (before)
      "Constraint: every one of your ideas must be carried out [constraint]. Within that constraint, make the ideas as informative as possible." The eight constraints are listed below.
      Critique and revise (whole prompt)
      The question, the five plain-prompt ideas from the matching call, then: "A senior expert reviewing them says they are the ideas everyone in the field would propose first. Propose five replacement ideas (analyses, measurements, or experiments) that are substantially less obvious but still concrete, scientifically sound, and feasible for a research lab. Do not repeat or lightly modify the ideas above."
      Poem and random words, follow-up (before)
      "Here is a piece of material that is unrelated to the research question below. Each of your ideas must draw on this material in some way (for example through an analogy, a structure, a principle, or a pattern it suggests), while still being a concrete, scientifically sound way to address the question." The material follows in tags: one of eight public-domain poems, or sixty random dictionary words, the same texts as in the earlier study.
      Future retrospective (whole prompt)
      The question, then: "It is 2036. Looking back, five landmark papers each settled this question in a way that surprised the field, because of how they approached it. For each paper, describe what it measured, analyzed, or did, as a concrete idea a research lab could pursue today."

      Each call was isolated: the model could not search the web or run code, saw only a generic one-line instruction, and had to return its ideas in a fixed machine-readable format. Technically, all calls ran through the Claude command-line tool with no tools, a neutral system prompt ("You are a helpful assistant."), no user settings, an empty working directory and structured output. A call that returned the wrong number of ideas or a missing intermediate list was re-sent unchanged, up to twice (three attempts in all); 13 such retries occurred across the 432 calls, almost all with Opus 5.5. No judging batch was stopped by a safety filter (0 ratings missing).
